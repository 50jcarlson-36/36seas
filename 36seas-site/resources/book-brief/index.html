The 36seas field notes

Before the chapters,
find the promise.

A practical writing brief helps you decide what belongs in the book—and gives the next session a useful starting point.

On this page

The readerThe promiseThe shapeThe next session

01 / The reader

Write for someone you can describe.

Begin with the person most likely to value the book. For professional nonfiction, name the situation they are in and the question they need to resolve. For fiction, consider the kind of experience you are inviting them into: suspense, wonder, recognition, unease.

Specificity is a tool for decisions. It does not prohibit unexpected readers. It helps you judge whether an opening, example, scene, or explanation serves the book you intend to write.

02 / The promise

Describe the reason to keep reading.

Write a provisional sentence: “This book is for ___, who wants ___.” Then make it less generic. A promise can describe a practical change, an argument worth considering, or a compelling journey. It should give you something to test against the manuscript.

Avoid trying to summarize every detail. A useful brief is short enough to return to before a writing session. It should remind you what the book is doing when the draft starts pulling in several directions.

03 / The shape

Map a sequence of work.

List the major movements, questions, or chapters. Ask what the reader needs to encounter first and what each part makes possible next. The first map is a working hypothesis, not a contract with the final book.

Mark what you know, what needs exploration, and what needs verification. This separates drafting from research and prevents a missing fact from stopping every kind of progress.

04 / The next session

Choose a small finished deliverable.

End the brief with one task you can complete: a scene, an opening argument, a section outline, or a revision of an existing passage. Define what “done for now” means before you begin.

Keep discoveries in a note beside the manuscript. Revise the brief when your understanding changes. A framework earns its place by helping the book move forward, not by demanding that every early assumption survive.
